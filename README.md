## 1. Program Overview

### Purpose

Leadership Academy takes proven leadership and business books and turns them into a practical classroom experience for small business owners. The goal is application, not book reports. Each book is broken down into its core frameworks, named examples, and actionable advice, then paired with discussion questions and assignments that ask participants to use the ideas inside their own businesses, with their own teams, on a real timeline.

### Audience

The primary audience is **small business owners**: people who are simultaneously the leader, the operator, and often the bottleneck in their organizations. Materials are written with their constraints in mind:

- Limited time. Assignments are sized to fit a real calendar (for example, the *Tribal Leadership* assignment was deliberately condensed from 30 days to 14).
- Small teams. Exercises assume the owner can personally observe and influence most of the people in the business.
- The owner as the lever. Many exercises end with a "mirror" question, because in a small business the culture usually starts and stops with the owner.

### Format

- **Facilitated class sessions** built around assigned reading from each book.
- **Structured breakdowns** of each section (examples, concepts, takeaways, practical advice) that the facilitator uses to lead discussion.
- **Discussion questions** grounded in the participants' own businesses.
- **Take-home assignments** with defined deliverables and timelines.
- **Reference materials**: glossaries (full and condensed), Word handouts, emailed summaries.
- **Interactive tools**: standalone HTML exercises used in or after class (for example, the Circles of Control tool).

### Core Program Philosophy

1. **Theory → diagnosis → action → reflection.** Every module should move participants from understanding a framework to using it as a diagnostic tool on their business, taking one concrete action, and reflecting honestly on their own role.
2. **Specific beats general.** Assignments ask for exact words, named people, and concrete actions (not "encourage collaboration" but "assign two named people to co-own a named deliverable that neither can do alone").
3. **The owner goes first.** Culture change starts with the person at the top; self-assessment is built into every module.
4. **Examples carry the lesson.** Named stories from each book (Viktor Frankl, Griffin Hospital, the $2 hot dog, the Wright Brothers) are the backbone of class discussion because they make abstract frameworks memorable.

## 3. Instructional Design Standards

These are the patterns that have worked in building this curriculum. New modules should follow them so the materials stay consistent.

### 3.1 Section Breakdown Template

Each section, part, chapter, or habit is broken down with these components:

| Component | What It Contains |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Core framework** | The central idea of the section in plain language. |
| **Signature example(s)** | The named stories, people, and companies the author uses, told with enough detail to retell in class. |
| **Key concepts & terminology** | Named models, diagrams, and vocabulary (e.g., Circle of Influence, triads, Golden Circle). |
| **Data points** | Any statistics, percentages, dates, or research cited by the author. |
| **Major takeaways** | The 3–5 things a participant should walk away believing. |
| **Practical advice** | What to actually do differently on Monday. |

### 3.2 Discussion Question Standard

- **Three questions per section** (the established pattern for *The 7 Habits*).
- Each question should be anchored to a **specific framework or example** from the reading.
- Each question should point the participant at **their own business**, their team, their calendar, or their own language.
- Prefer questions that require **evidence** ("look at your calendar and bank statement from the last 30 days") over questions that invite opinions.
- At least one question per set should turn the lens on the **owner's own behavior**.

### 3.3 Assignment Standard

- **Objective**: one sentence on what the participant will diagnose or change.
- **Parts with day ranges** that fit the available window (current standard: **14 days**).
- **A concrete deliverable per part** (a one-page chart, a short written plan, one honest paragraph).
- **Actions drawn directly from the book's own tools** (e.g., the leverage points for a specific stage transition).
- **A success signal**: what language or behavior change would show it's working.
- **A mirror question** at the end: honest self-reflection tied to the section's key story.
- **Optional in-class share-out**, anonymized (no employee names), to surface shared blind spots.

### 3.4 Glossary Standard

- **Full glossary**: every named saying, strategy, framework, and ritual, grouped into thematic sections, with rich definitions and context.
- **Condensed glossary**: every entry retained, definitions trimmed to **1–3 sentences**. Roughly a third of the length; used as a quick-reference handout.

### 3.5 Formats & Distribution

| Format | Used For |
|--------|----------|
| Structured written summaries (Markdown / chat) | Facilitator prep; source for everything else |
| Microsoft Word (.docx) | Class handouts and reference documents (styled headings, bolded terms, accent colors) |
| Standalone HTML | Interactive class exercises (no dependencies; open in any browser) |
| Email | Distributing summaries to participants |

### 3.6 Source Material Notes

- *The 7 Habits* is taught from a **physical copy of the 2020 30th Anniversary Edition**. Page numbers for that edition could not be found online and need to be filled in from the book.
- Breakdowns were produced from knowledge of the texts plus web research. Specific details should be spot-checked against the physical books before they go on a slide or handout (see [Section 14](#14-source-notes--verification-checklist)).

## 4. Module: *Tribal Leadership*

**Authors:** Dave Logan, John King, Halee Fischer-Wright
**Research base:** A 10-year study of roughly 24,000 people across more than two dozen organizations.
**Structure:** Four parts. Part 1 lays out the system; Part 2 walks through leading people stage by stage (Chapters 4–8); Part 3 covers stabilizing Stage Four (Chapters 9–11); Part 4 covers Stage Five (Chapter 12).
**Module status:** All four parts broken down; Part 2 assignment built and in use.

### 4.1 Stage Quick Reference

| Stage | Mantra | Share of Workplace Tribes | Relationship Pattern | What It Sounds / Looks Like |
|-------|--------|---------------------------|----------------------|------------------------------|
| **1** | "Life sucks" | ~2% | Alienated; despairing hostility | Feels simultaneously "special" and cut off; free to act on any impulse |
| **2** | "My life sucks" | ~25% | Passive, resigned, commiserating | Sarcasm, Dilbert cartoons on the cubicle wall, "hanging on for the weekend," little initiative or accountability |
| **3** | "I'm great (and you're not)" | ~49% | **Dyads**: hub-and-spoke one-on-one relationships | Lone warriors, information hoarding, competitive put-downs disguised as jokes, "nobody else is as competent," never enough time |
| **4** | "We're great (and they're not)" | ~22% | **Triads**: three-person relationships where each person owns the quality of the relationship between the other two | "We" language, shared values, trust assumed from the start |
| **5** | "Life is great" | <2% | Ever-expanding networks with anyone whose values resonate | No "they"; "innocent wonderment"; the competitor is the problem itself, not another company |

**Two markers identify any stage:** the **language** people use and the **structure of their relationships**.

---

### 4.2 Part 1: The Tribal Leadership System

**Core framework.** Organizations are networks of **tribes**: groups of about **20–150 people** who know each other well enough that they'd stop and say hello on the street. **Culture is the product of the language people use and the behaviors that come with it.** A **Tribal Leader** is someone who focuses on upgrading tribal culture.

#### Signature Example: Griffin Hospital (Derby, Connecticut)

The central case study of Part 1, showing a tribe moving through the stages:

- **Stage 2 (mid-1980s):** A community survey found **32%** of nearby residents named Griffin as the hospital they would *avoid*. Staff were apathetic, passive, and carried a victim mentality. The pivotal moment: VP **Bill Powanda's** father-in-law was admitted with an inoperable stomach ulcer, and staff were rude and indifferent to him for an entire month, even though he was an executive's family member.
- **The turn:** **Patrick Charmel** (later CEO) and Bill Powanda began engaging employees individually, asking their opinions on real decisions like patient treatment protocols and new building designs. People felt valued and their focus shifted from their own insecurities to patient care.
- **Stage 3 conflict:** Individualism surfaced at the top. The then-CEO clashed with COO Charmel over resource allocation, treating growth as his personal mission ("I'm great"). Charmel was fired, and employees protested for his return.
- **Stage 4/5:** After Charmel returned, the tribe stabilized around shared values with relatively hands-off leadership. Griffin became an award-winning, patient-centered hospital, was named by *Fortune* as one of the best companies to work for, and now hosts other hospitals studying its model.

#### Signature Example: Amgen (Stage 5)

- In the 1990s, when asked who their main competitor was, Amgen employees named **diseases** (inflammatory disease, arthritis, obesity, Parkinson's), not rival companies.
- An engineer talked about billions in patent revenue in terms of new diseases they might now be able to cure, not competitive advantage.
- The authors describe the mood as **"innocent wonderment."**
- Amgen oscillated between Stage 4 and Stage 5 like waves on a beach: spiking to Stage 5 during breakthroughs, returning to Stage 4 in normal operations.
- Amgen codified **eight core values** through interviews with **400 people**: science-based inquiry, competition, teamwork, creating value, ensuring quality, collaboration, communication, and being ethical.

#### Key Insights

1. **Tribes advance one stage at a time.** Trying to jump from Stage 2 to Stage 4 creates instability.
2. **Language and relationship structure** are the two diagnostic markers.
3. **The Tribal Leader upgrades culture one person at a time** until the whole tribe levels up.
4. **Culture determines success more than strategy.**

### 4.3 Part 2: Your Journey as a Leader (Chapters 4–8)

**Core framework.** Part 2 is the practical heart of the book. It walks through each stage: how to recognize it, how to coach people out of it, and what success looks like at each transition. It builds to the **Tribal Leadership Epiphany**, the Stage 3 → 4 gap that the authors call "Grand Canyon huge."

#### Chapter 4: Stage One, On the Verge of a Meltdown

- **Frank Jordan:** Grew up exposed to gang culture and crime; became a Tribal Leader through the Boys & Girls Clubs and helped others escape Stage One. His story shows the Stage One cocktail of feeling "special" (unrecognized gifts) and alienated at the same time.
- **Chicago prosecutors** describing the Stage One world: broken families, gang initiation, cycles of incarceration.
- **Mike, owner of Design For You (Chicago):** Employs former gang members, ex-convicts, and people recovering from heavy drug use. Uses a "horse whisperer" protocol: if someone relapses into despairing hostility, he pushes them out of the organization but never gives up on them permanently.

**Leverage points, Stage 1 → 2:** Set boundaries but never abandon people. Encourage them to go where the action is (lunches, social functions, meetings) and notice that life works for some people. Encourage cutting ties with people who reinforce "life sucks." The goal is the shift from "life sucks" to "my life sucks," which is progress because it admits improvement is possible.

#### Chapter 5: Stage Two, Disconnected and Disengaged

- **The DMV and Dilbert culture:** Government-office stereotypes, *Office Space*, and **Scott Adams'** Dilbert as the mascot of Stage Two. "I'd rather be fishing" mugs and "Hanging on for the weekend" posters.
- **Career regression:** Many workers start their first job with Stage Three ambition, get beaten down by cynical coworkers and bad bosses, and settle into commiserating about how accurately Dilbert depicts their lives.
- **Education's oscillation:** School keeps people cycling between Stages 2 and 3 (each new course shows you what you don't know; each exam lets you prove mastery). Most people graduate somewhere in the 2–3 range.

**Leverage points, Stage 2 → 3:** Work **one-on-one**, not culture-wide. Zero tolerance for Stage One behavior. Tell people they're valued. Help them build individual competencies. Point them to mentors at Stage Three or above (dyadic relationships that pull them up). The signal: "this place is hopeless" becomes "I'm good at this."

#### Chapter 6: Stage Three, The Wild, Wild West

The dominant culture in **49%** of workplaces, and the most extensive chapter in Part 2.

- **Who lives here:** Doctors, professors, attorneys, salespeople, executives. Individually ambitious and high-performing, but perpetually frustrated by lack of time and support.
- **Dyads:** A manager of ten runs ten separate one-on-one relationships instead of a team. Knowledge is power, so information is hoarded.
- **The two-stage bonus:** When self-assessing, people add two stages; most Stage Three people believe they're at Stage Five.
- **Archetypes:** The overworked executive who complains no one else is competent; Gary Cole's boss character in *Office Space*.
- **Self-help trap:** Most business self-help books unknowingly operate at Stage Three: they teach individual excellence and personal victory but never the cultural shift beyond it.
- **Limiting behaviors** rooted in the ego-driven "I" system: controlling information flow, building political alliances for personal advantage, measuring success with individual metrics, and treating colleagues as tools or obstacles.

**Leverage points, Stage 3 → 4:** The hardest transition. You can't just teach triading to a Stage Three person; without real transformation it reads as self-promotion. Encourage projects that require genuine partnership, point out the superior results of Stage Four tribes, and show the person that they've built their work so nothing functions without them, which is both their greatest source of pride and the ceiling on their impact.

#### Chapter 7: The Tribal Leadership Epiphany

**Signature example: Bob Tobias and the NTEU.** Tobias was general counsel of what became the National Treasury Employees Union. He sued President Richard Nixon and won a **$533 million** judgment, a massive Stage Three personal win. Elected union president in **1983**, he was asked by a reporter what he planned to do now, and realized he had no idea. That question triggered his epiphany.

**The five parts of the epiphany:**

| Part | Question | What Happened for Tobias |
|------|----------|--------------------------|
| 1 | **"What have I achieved?"** | Despite adversarial victories, his real impact on members' lives was minimal. His wins were personal, not tribal. |
| 2 | **"How can I fix this?"** | He preached collaboration and interest-based bargaining; his "badass" staff ignored him. He was still trying to fix a Stage Three problem with Stage Three behavior. |
| 3 | **"What's the real goal?"** | Real impact on a large group. He combined his Stage Three competence with what mattered (workers' rights) and found that speaking for thousands gave him more power, not less. |
| 4 | **"How does a Tribal Leader use power?"** | Tough calls, even removing people, can serve the tribe (the authors reference Jack Welch's "Neutron Jack" years at GE). The difference is intent: self-interest vs. the collective. |
| 5 | **"I am because we are"** | Language shifts from "I" to "we." The union grew and formed a major partnership with federal leadership. |

**Additional context:**
- For some, **9/11** catalyzed the epiphany; for most it's a series of small realizations.
- **Ken Wilber** told the authors that psychology, worldview, and conversations tend to advance in stages together.
- **Trust:** At Stage Three, trust is earned (and re-earned). At Stage Four, it's assumed. Stage Three people often refused the researchers' interview requests; Stage Four and Five people said yes because the project sounded important.
- **The irony:** Everything Stage Three people chase (esteem, respect, loyalty, legacy, enduring success) only becomes achievable at Stage Four.

#### Chapter 8: Stage Four, Establishing Tribal Leadership

**Signature example: David Kelley and IDEO.** IDEO designed the first Apple mouse, the stand-up squeeze toothpaste bottle, the Steelcase Leap Chair, and the Palm V. A mentor encouraged Kelley to write how he hoped to be eulogized, a values-clarifying exercise that shaped IDEO's culture. IDEO began as Kelley and ten friends; nine were still there thirty years later. At Stanford he's known by his title; at IDEO he's "a member of the team."

**Three paths into Stage Four:**

1. **The Tribal Seed.** A group of like-minded friends asks how they can make money together. Relationships come before the business model. (IDEO is the archetype.)
2. **The Stage Four Niche.** Find other late-Stage-Three / early-Stage-Four people inside your organization and build a department or project that operates at a higher level.
3. **The Technical Project.** Join a project too big for one person, which forces genuine collaboration.

**Additional example: the bead maker ("tribal antennae").** A woman named Rueve, stringing together other people's beads, was asked for pieces by Michael Jordan. She spent months learning real bead-making; then Scottie Pippen and Dennis Rodman asked for her work, and the business grew around the values of vitality, quality, and passion. The lesson: listen for contributions from people not yet in your tribe. *(Verify details against the text; see Section 14.)*

#### Part 2 Takeaways

Each transition needs different tools and language. The most common mistake is applying Stage Three tactics (individual excellence, competition, self-help) to problems that need Stage Four solutions (shared values, triads, noble cause). The biggest decision any person or company faces is whether to stay comfortable at Three or make the leap to Four. Tobias's summary, paraphrased: if your goal is to win, keep doing what you're doing; if your goal is a larger impact, you have to build relationships and work with others.

### 4.4 Part 3: Owning Tribal Leadership, Stabilizing Stage Four (Chapters 9–11)

#### Chapter 9: Core Values and a Noble Cause

- **Core value:** a principle without which life wouldn't be worth living. **Noble cause:** the shared direction the tribe is shooting toward. Values are what you **stand for**; the noble cause is what you **shoot for**.
- **Amgen under CEO Gordon Binder:** Employees at every level felt a personal, almost spiritual connection to curing disease. A middle manager started the project that surfaced Amgen's eight core values, which then shaped hiring, promotions, and daily decisions. Successful organizations commit to values publicly even before they seem to be in a position to.
- **Warning:** You can't maintain values by purging people who don't align; that regresses culture. Instead, tell value-laden stories that invite others to share theirs.
- **Alignment ≠ agreement.** Move in the same direction without stifling independent thinking.

#### Chapter 10: Triads and Stage Four Networking

- **Triad:** a three-person relationship in which each person takes responsibility for the quality of the relationship between the other two. Contrast with Stage Three **dyads**, driven by personal agendas and information hoarding.
- **Darla Longo, Vice Chair of CB Richard Ellis:** Hosts an annual September real estate party (by 2006, the entire fifteenth floor of San Francisco's Clift Hotel, 300+ guests). Instead of working the room, she spends the night introducing pairs of people based on shared values and mutual interest ("you both care deeply about integrity... you could save each other time"), then moves on. People talk about her glowingly after she leaves: the praise Stage Three people chase but can only get by letting go of ego.
- **Trust:** Stage Three requires earned trust; Stage Four assumes it.

#### Chapter 11: A Tribal Leader's Guide to Strategy

The strategy model flows from the center outward:

```
Core Values + Noble Cause
        ↓
   Outcomes  (what we want)
        ↓
    Assets   (what we have)
        ↓
  Behaviors  (who will do what)
```

- **Keep the three conversations separate.** Most strategies fail because tribes try to have all three at once or skip one.
- **Outcome vs. goal:** A goal implies current failure; an outcome describes a present state of success that keeps expanding. Outcomes must be measurable and time-bound.
- **Assets** are often invisible to insiders. Ask: "What do we have a knack for doing better than anyone else?"
- When everything connects, the tribe feels a surge of excitement, and a tribe on the 3/4 border typically moves solidly into "we're great."

### 4.5 Part 4: Toward Vital Work Communities, Stage Five (Chapter 12)

- The shortest part (one chapter). The authors hadn't planned to include a fifth stage; Amgen forced them to recognize it.
- Language moves from "we're great (and they're not)" to **"life is great."** No "they."
- **Amgen in the 1990s** is the pivotal example (competitors named as diseases; "innocent wonderment").
- **Stage Five is a cycle, not a destination.** After a burst of history-making work, the tribe settles back to Stage Four. The leader's job is to recognize and seize the conditions (breakthroughs, market moments) that catapult the tribe back up.
- **Griffin Hospital** and **IDEO** are cited as reaching Stage Five at least periodically.
- At Stage Five, the tribe becomes a **magnet** for resources, partners, and talent.

**Parts 3–4 takeaway:** The summit isn't beating a competitor; it's a culture so grounded in values and purpose that competition becomes irrelevant. Tools: surface values through storytelling, build triads instead of dyads, anchor strategy in a noble cause, and create conditions for "life is great" breakthroughs.

### 4.6 Assignment: Tribal Stage Audit & Upgrade Plan (14 Days) ✅ *Current version*

> Assigned with the Part 2 reading. Condensed from an original 30-day version to fit a 14-day window.

**Objective:** Diagnose where your team is using the Part 2 framework and design one real intervention.

**Part A: The Listening Exercise (Days 1–5)**
Over a normal work week, listen to how your team talks: in meetings, messages, and casual conversation. Pick **3–5 people** and for each one note:
- A couple of phrases they commonly use (exact words)
- Their stage assessment (1–5) and why
- Whether they mostly relate to you one-on-one or connect independently with teammates

Then do the same for yourself, remembering the authors' warning about the **two-stage bonus**.
**Deliverable:** A simple one-page chart of your observations.

**Part B: One-Person Upgrade Plan (Days 6–10)**
Pick the one person whose stage upgrade would create the biggest ripple effect. Write a short plan that includes:
- Their current stage and the evidence for it
- The transition you're targeting (e.g., Stage 2 → 3)
- **Two specific actions** pulled directly from the book's leverage points for that transition. Be concrete: not "encourage collaboration" but "assign Mike and Lisa to co-own the proposal for Client X because neither can deliver it alone."
- What language shift or behavior change would tell you it's working

**Part C: The Mirror Question (Days 11–14)**
One honest paragraph: Bob Tobias's epiphany began when he realized his victories were personal, not tribal. Where in your business are you still operating as a Stage Three lone warrior? What's one thing you could change about how you lead this month to start closing that gap?

**Facilitator notes:**
- The real learning is in **Part A**. Most small business owners have never simply *listened* to their team's language.
- Parts B and C make sure they act on what they heard.
- **Optional in-class share-out:** Have participants share Part A findings without naming employees. Hearing others describe similar patterns helps people recognize their own blind spots.

#### Archived: Original 30-Day Version

Kept for cohorts with a longer window.

- **Part A: The Listening Exercise (first week).** Spend 3 business days listening, unannounced, to team language (meetings, Slack, hallway, lunch). For each person, or 5–7 key people on larger teams: 2–3 exact phrases, whether relationships are dyadic (you as hub) or networked, and an honest stage rating. Assess yourself too. *Deliverable:* chart of names, language, relationship structure, stage.
- **Part B: Identify Your Biggest Opportunity.** A short paragraph each: (1) the dominant stage of the business and the evidence; (2) the one person whose upgrade would ripple furthest, and why; (3) where you personally operate as a Stage Three lone warrior.
- **Part C: Design a 30-Day Intervention.** For that one person: current stage and evidence, target transition, **three** concrete actions from the book's leverage points (e.g., "pair Sarah with James on the Henderson project because it's too big for either alone"), and how you'll measure the shift.
- **Part D: The Mirror Question.** If your whole team described your leadership honestly and anonymously, would they describe a Stage Three lone warrior or a Stage Four tribal leader? What one thing could you change about how you communicate this month?
- *Why it works:* Part A builds the listening muscle; Part B forces honest self-assessment; Part C creates accountability tied to a real person; Part D returns to the Tobias epiphany: the culture upgrade starts with the owner.

## 5. Module: *Start with Why*

**Author:** Simon Sinek
**Structure:** 6 parts, 14 chapters
**Module status:** Chapter-by-chapter structure and business examples mapped. Discussion questions, assignment, and glossary not yet built.

### 5.1 Core Concepts

| Concept | Summary |
|---------|---------|
| **The Golden Circle** | Three concentric rings: **Why** (purpose, cause, belief) at the center, **How** (the process or values that bring the why to life), **What** (the products or services). Inspiring leaders and organizations communicate from the inside out; most communicate from the outside in. |
| **Manipulation vs. Inspiration** | "Carrots and sticks" (price cuts, promotions, fear, aspirational messaging, peer pressure) can drive transactions but not loyalty. Inspiration, rooted in a clear why, drives lasting loyalty. |
| **The Biology** | The Golden Circle maps to the brain: the limbic brain (feelings, trust, decision-making, no capacity for language) corresponds to Why and How; the neocortex (rational thought, language) corresponds to What. That's why people often "go with their gut" and struggle to explain why. |
| **Clarity, Discipline, Consistency** | Clarity of Why, discipline of How, consistency of What. Authenticity comes from alignment across all three. |
| **Trust** | Trust emerges when an organization's values and beliefs are visible and consistent, and when it takes care of its own people first. |
| **Law of Diffusion of Innovations** | The adoption curve (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards). Mass-market success comes from first winning the people who believe what you believe. |
| **Why-Type / How-Type Partnership** | Visionaries who carry the cause pair with builders who know how to make it real. |
| **The Celery Test** | A decision filter: if you know your Why, it's obvious which options to take (the celery and rice milk) and which to leave (the tempting items that don't fit), and people who see your choices instantly understand what you believe. |
| **When Why Goes Fuzzy / Split Happens** | Success and founder departures are the biggest threats to a clear Why; organizations drift into managing What. |

### 5.2 Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown and Business Examples

#### Part 1: A World That Doesn't Start With Why

**Chapter 1: Assume You Know**

## 6. Module: *Unreasonable Hospitality*

**Author:** Will Guidara
**Publisher:** Optimism Press, 2022
**Front matter:** Opens with a letter from **Simon Sinek** (a direct bridge to the *Start with Why* module)
**Structure:** 20 chapters plus epilogue
**Module status:** Full synopsis, chapter breakdown, full glossary (40+ entries), and condensed glossary complete; condensed glossary exported to styled Word (.docx). Discussion questions and assignment not yet built.

### 6.1 Core Premise

The book is Guidara's account of how he and chef **Daniel Humm** turned **Eleven Madison Park (EMP)** from a well-regarded New York brasserie into the **#1 restaurant in the world** (World's 50 Best Restaurants, **2017**). It's really a philosophy of service, leadership, and human connection that applies to any industry.

- **Central argument:** Hospitality, making people feel seen and cared for, is the most undervalued competitive advantage in business.
- **Service vs. hospitality:** Service is the technical execution (right order, on time). Hospitality is how you make someone *feel* during it. "Service is black and white; hospitality is color."
- **Why "unreasonable":** Going so far beyond expectations that it seems almost irrational creates loyalty, word of mouth, and emotional resonance that marketing can't buy.

### 6.2 Guidara's Journey

- Grew up in the business; his father, **Frank Guidara**, was a prominent restaurant-company executive, and Will learned early that restaurants are about far more than food. His father's emphasis on **intentionality** runs through the book.
- Studied at **Cornell's School of Hotel Administration**.
- At **26**, became general manager of EMP (then owned by **Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group**) alongside Humm.
- Over the following decade-plus, EMP earned four stars from the *New York Times*, three Michelin stars, and the #1 World's 50 Best ranking.
- He's candid about the personal cost, the surprising emptiness at the summit, and the eventual end of his partnership with Humm.

### 6.5 Leadership & Team Culture Themes

- **Hospitality starts with your people.** You can't deliver extraordinary hospitality to guests without first delivering it to your team: staff meals, outings, genuine care for employees' lives.
- **The pre-meal meeting** as the day's most important culture ritual: stories, shout-outs, connection, never rote.
- **Trust people to make decisions** on the floor (comping a dish, sending a treat) without asking permission. Rigid systems kill hospitality.
- **Being present** on the floor, not hiding in the office, signals that every night matters.
- **A 50/50 partnership** between the dining room and the kitchen. In any business, the "product people" and the "experience people" should be equal partners.
- **The pursuit of #1:** relentless reinvention, relationship-building, big risks, real personal sacrifice, and a surprising sense of emptiness on arrival.

### 6.6 Principles That Transfer Outside Restaurants

1. **Read the guest** (customer, client). Notice what people need, not just what they ask for.
2. **Create a culture of "yes."** The instinct should be toward generosity and problem-solving.
3. **Invest disproportionately in "non-core" experiences.** Greetings, follow-ups, packaging, handwritten notes often matter more than the core product.
4. **Celebrate and ritualize.** Rituals create belonging; celebration is a strategic tool.
5. **Be willing to be unreasonable.** Reasonable people do sensible things; unreasonable people create magic.

**The emotional heart of the book:** people want to feel seen.

### 6.7 Condensed Glossary (all entries)

#### Core Philosophy

- **"Unreasonable Hospitality"**: A commitment to making people feel cared for that stretches beyond what seems sensible. "Unreasonable" was originally used to shut Guidara down; he reclaimed it as a rallying cry.
- **"Service Is Black and White; Hospitality Is Color"**: Service is technical competence; hospitality is the warmth and connection around it. You need both.
- **"The Hospitality Economy"**: Over 75% of U.S. GDP comes from service. Whatever your industry, you're in the business of serving people, and how you make them feel is your edge.
- **"Luxury Means Giving More; Hospitality Means Being More Thoughtful"**: Luxury is lavishness; hospitality is intention. A $2 hot dog served with care beats a $200 gesture done without thought.
- **"Hospitality Is a Selfish Pleasure"**: Making people feel good feels great for the giver, too. It's energizing, not draining.

#### Operational Rules & Frameworks

- **The Rule of 95/5**: Manage 95% of your business down to the penny. Spend the last 5% "foolishly" on surprise-and-delight moments; it's the smartest money you'll spend.
- **The One-Inch Rule**: Literally, set plates down so gently there's no sound; control the final inch. Metaphorically, follow through to the very last step. A careless finish can ruin great work.
- **"The Last 5%"**: The gap between excellent and transcendent lives in the final details, where most people get lazy. *(See verification note in Section 14.)*
- **"Legends"**: EMP's term for unexpected, personalized gestures (the hot dog, the beach). Created by listening, executed with flair.
- **"Dreamweavers"**: The dedicated role for conceiving and coordinating Legends. Systemized spontaneity.
- **"Plus One" / Plus One Cards**: Always look for a way to give a little more. The cards held answers to common guest questions so servers could over-deliver effortlessly.
- **The Pre-Meal Meeting**: A daily 30-minute pre-service meeting turned from logistics into the day's key culture ritual.
- **The Ownership Program**: Team members volunteered to "own" parts of the experience (music, flowers, water service), deepening engagement and creativity.
- **"The Black Book"**: A guest database of preferences, allergies, past visits, and offhand comments, so a dessert you mentioned once could appear on your next visit unasked.

#### Leadership & Team Culture

- **"Make the Charitable Assumption"** (from Danny Meyer): Assume the best of people, especially when they're behaving badly. Ask "Is everything okay?" before judging.
- **"One Size Fits One"** (from Richard Coraine): Every guest and team member is different; serve people the way *they* want to be served.
- **"Criticize the Behavior, Not the Person"**: Part of Guidara's five feedback rules: (1) criticize behavior, not the person; (2) praise in public, criticize in private; (3) praise with emotion, criticize without; (4) be willing to criticize yourself; (5) don't get defensive.
- **"Their Perception Is Our Reality"**: A guest's experience, even if technically wrong, is the reality to honor.
- **"Being Right Is Irrelevant"**: The need to be right is the enemy of connection. Guest sends back a perfect steak? Apologize and cook a new one.
- **"Don't Cannonball. Ease Into the Pool."**: In a new organization, understand the culture before changing it.
- **"Constant, Gentle Pressure"**: Danny Meyer's version of kaizen: steady, incremental improvement over dramatic overhauls.
- **"Athletic Hospitality"**: Always looking for a win, on offense (creating moments) or defense (recovering from mistakes).
- **"Be the Swan"**: Grace on the surface, frantic paddling underneath. Guests never see the scramble.
- **The Deep Breathing Club (DBC)**: Staff reminded each other to breathe in high-stress moments; three DBC events earned a t-shirt. Meltdowns dropped.
- **"Ask for Help" (The Lapel Touch)**: A discreet signal to request support during service. Asking for help is strength.
- **"Swap Sides"**: In a disagreement, argue each other's position to move from "my idea" to "what's right."
- **"That's Important to Me"**: After swapping sides, whoever says this gets their way. The person who cares more wins.
- **"Tend to Yourself First"**: You can't pour from an empty cup; leaders must restore themselves.

#### Culture & Language

- **"Language Creates Culture"**: The phrases you coin and repeat become the organization's DNA.
- **The Four Core Values**: Excellence, Hospitality, Creativity, Passion. Excellence and Hospitality were in tension (perfection can turn cold); the answer was integrative, both/and thinking.
- **The Eleven Words (The Miles Davis Roadmap)**: Inspired by Miles Davis's reinventions; eleven guiding words hung in the kitchen as EMP's creative north star. "Collaborative" stood out most.
- **"Nobility in Service"**: Serving others is dignified and important; reject the bias that service work is lesser.
- **"Restaurant-Smart" vs. "Corporate-Smart"**: Restaurant-smart is nimble and passionate but lacks infrastructure; corporate-smart has systems and profit but can stifle connection. Be both.
- **"No Aspect of Your Business Should Be Off-Limits to Reevaluation"**: "We've always done it this way" is never enough.
- **"Run Toward What You Want Instead of Running Away from What You Don't Want"**: Aspiration, not avoidance.
- **"Start with What You Want to Achieve, Instead of Limiting Yourself to What's Realistic"**: Dream first, reverse-engineer later. EMP went from #50 to #1 this way.

#### Guest Experience Strategies

- **"Act As If" (The Critic of the Night)**: One table a night treated as the critic for a year; result: four stars.
- **"Is There Anything You Don't Like?"**: Ask about preferences, not just allergies, and go first with your own ("I hate sea urchin"). Offer a piece of yourself and people open up.
- **The Cognac Bottle**: A full bottle arrived with the check. Generosity at the most transactional moment.
- **The Kitchen Tour for Every Guest**: A VIP privilege made universal.
- **The Beer and Pretzel Course**: Playful, irreverent, and a signal not to take yourself too seriously.
- **The $2 Hot Dog**: Overheard, bought, plated on fine china. Cost: $2. Impact: priceless.
- **The Beach in the Dining Room**: Sand in a private dining room for a couple who missed their beach trip.

#### Hiring & Team Building

- **"Be as Unreasonable in How You Build Your Team as You Are in How You Build Your Product"**: Hire slowly, intentionally, with obsessive care.
- **"The Best Interview Technique Is No Technique at All"**: Have a real conversation; look for curiosity, passion, integrity, respect.
- **Hiring in Teams**: Bring in small aligned groups who act as cultural catalysts.
- **"Everyone Starts as a Kitchen Server"**: New hires absorb the culture and the food before facing guests.
- **"All It Takes for Something Extraordinary to Happen Is One Person with Enthusiasm"** (from Richard Coraine): Find those people and give them room.
- **"Identify the Strengths of Your People, No Matter How Buried"**: Leadership is excavation.
- **"Choose Worthy Rivals"**: Benchmark against those who do something better than you.
- **"Tap Into Their Passions, Then Give Them the Keys"**: Find what people care about, then give them ownership.

#### Mindset & Personal Philosophy

- **"Take What You Do Seriously, But Don't Take Yourself Too Seriously"**: Serious about craft, playful about ego.
- **Maya Angelou's line on how people remember the way you made them feel**: The book's philosophical north star.
- **"Fads Fade, But the Human Desire to Be Cared For Never Goes Away"**: Build strategy around what's permanent.
- **"The Food, the Service, and the Design Are Simply Ingredients in the Recipe of Human Connection"**: The real product is connection.
- **Intentionality**: Inherited from his father. Every decision, from a greeting to a meeting, made with clear purpose.
- **"Don't Go to Bed Angry"**: Address conflict promptly; people want to be heard more than agreed with.
- **"You've Got to Start by Listening"**: Even when you disagree. Listening is the foundation of trust.
- **"Name for Yourself Why Your Work Matters"**: Without a purpose you can articulate, you can't sustain the energy excellence requires.
- **"Saying Sorry Doesn't Mean You're Wrong"**: Apologizing is hospitality, not an admission of fault.
- **"Criticism Is an Invitation to Have Your Perspective Challenged"**: Growth comes from genuinely considering another view and deciding again.
- **The 1% Improvement Philosophy**: Improve every element by 1% and the cumulative effect is transformational.
- **"Making Magic in a World That Could Use More of It"**: The book's animating spirit.

### 7.3 Foundations: Paradigms and Principles (first ~70 pages)

Covey spends the opening section reframing personal effectiveness before introducing a single habit.

#### Character Ethic vs. Personality Ethic

- Covey studied roughly **200 years of American success literature (about 1776 to the mid-1970s)**.
- For roughly the first 150 years, up to about the **1920s**, the literature emphasized the **Character Ethic**: integrity, humility, patience, courage, justice, hard work.
- After the 1920s it shifted to the **Personality Ethic**: public image, influence techniques, positive mental attitude, social skills as shortcuts. *Being* good vs. *appearing* good.
- **The farm analogy:** You can cram for an exam, but you can't cram on a farm. Lasting results follow natural processes.

#### Covey's Son

One of Covey's sons was struggling socially, athletically, and academically. Covey and his wife tried every Personality Ethic technique (coaching, encouragement, positive thinking, sticking up for him), and nothing worked, because the boy sensed they saw him as inadequate. The problem was the **lens**, not the boy. When they changed how they saw him and valued his individuality, their behavior changed naturally and he began to flourish on his own timeline.
**Lesson:** Small changes come from working on behavior; significant changes come from working on paradigms.

#### Paradigms and Paradigm Shifts

- Borrowed from **Thomas Kuhn**, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*.
- We see the world not as it is but as we are. We confuse our perception with reality.
- **The subway story:** On a quiet Sunday-morning subway, a father sat with eyes closed while his children ran wild. When Covey finally said something, the man explained they'd just left the hospital where the children's mother had died about an hour earlier. Covey's irritation turned instantly to compassion. Nothing external changed; his map did. **The way we see the problem is often the problem.**

#### Principles vs. Values

- Principles (fairness, honesty, integrity, dignity, service, quality, patience, growth) work like natural laws, operating whether or not we believe in them.
- **Values are subjective and chosen; principles are objective.** A gang of thieves can share the value of loyalty without aligning with the principle of integrity.
- Effectiveness depends on how accurately your paradigms reflect principles.

#### The Maturity Continuum

**Dependence → Independence → Interdependence**

| Stage | Paradigm | Sounds Like |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| Dependence | **You** | "You take care of me; it's your fault." |
| Independence | **I** | "I can do it; I'm responsible." |
| Interdependence | **We** | "We can combine our talents and create something greater." |

Culture glorifies independence, but effectiveness lives in interdependence, and you can't skip stages. Habits 1–3 (Private Victory) build independence; Habits 4–6 (Public Victory) build interdependence; Habit 7 renews.

#### The P/PC Balance

- Aesop's **goose that laid the golden eggs**: the greedy farmer kills the goose to get all the eggs at once and finds nothing.
- **P = Production** (the eggs, the results). **PC = Production Capability** (the goose, the capacity that produces results).
- Applies to three kinds of assets: **physical** (a machine run 24/7 without maintenance), **financial**, and **human** (a manager who squeezes people for short-term results loses the best ones).
- **Effectiveness is balancing results with the capacity to keep producing them.**

#### Foundations Takeaways

1. Lasting effectiveness comes from character, not technique.
2. The way you see a problem is usually the deeper problem; shifting paradigms beats changing behavior.
3. True effectiveness balances results with the capacity to produce them.

### 7.4 Habit 1: Be Proactive

**Core framework.** Humans have four unique endowments: **self-awareness, imagination, conscience, and independent will**. Between stimulus and response lies a space where we choose. Proactivity means taking responsibility for our lives ("response-ability") instead of blaming circumstances, conditions, or conditioning.

#### The Three Deterministic Maps

| Map | Sounds Like |
|-----|-------------|
| **Genetic determinism** | "It's in my DNA. My grandparents were like this." |
| **Psychic determinism** | "It's how I was raised. My childhood made me this way." |
| **Environmental determinism** | "It's my boss, my spouse, the economy." |

All three rest on a stimulus-response model rooted in Pavlov's (and later Skinner's) behaviorism. Accepting any of them as final is choosing to let outside forces run your life.

#### Signature Example: Viktor Frankl

A psychiatrist imprisoned in Nazi death camps, stripped of everything, whose family was killed. Frankl discovered what he called "the last of the human freedoms": the ability to choose his attitude in any circumstance. He used his imagination to picture himself lecturing students after the war about what he'd learned. His experience became the basis of **logotherapy**.

#### Proactive vs. Reactive Language

| Reactive | Proactive |
|----------|-----------|
| "There's nothing I can do." | "Let's look at our alternatives." |
| "That's just the way I am." | "I can choose a different approach." |
| "He makes me so mad." | "I control my own feelings." |
| "I have to." / "I can't." | "I choose." |
| "I must." | "I prefer." |
| "If only…" | "I will." |

Language both **reveals** and **reinforces** the underlying paradigm.

#### Circle of Concern vs. Circle of Influence

- **Circle of Concern (outer):** everything you care about: health, the economy, problems at work, the weather, world events.
- **Circle of Influence (inner):** what you can actually do something about.
- Reactive people focus on the Circle of Concern, and their Circle of Influence **shrinks**. Proactive people focus on the Circle of Influence, and it **expands**.

**Three kinds of problems:**

| Type | Involves | Response |
|------|----------|----------|
| **Direct control** | Our own behavior | Change our habits |
| **Indirect control** | Other people's behavior | Change our methods of influence |
| **No control** | Things we can't change (e.g., the past) | Change our attitude |

> Class tool: [Circles of Control, Influence & Concern](#81-circles-of-control-influence--concern) (interactive HTML).

#### Other Examples & Ideas

- **The business executive:** A man in one of Covey's groups blamed his boss for everything. Covey challenged him to work his Circle of Influence: anticipate the boss's needs, understand his concerns, bring solutions instead of complaints. Over time the relationship transformed, not because the boss changed, but because he did.
- **Carry your own weather:** Proactive people bring their own internal climate and are value-driven; reactive people are emotional barometers that rise and fall with how others treat them.

#### The 30-Day Proactivity Test

For 30 days, work only in your Circle of Influence. Make small commitments and keep them. Be a light, not a judge. Be a model, not a critic. Be part of the solution, not the problem.

#### Takeaways

1. You are **response-able** for your life; behavior is a product of decisions, not conditions.
2. **Watch your language.** It's both diagnostic and corrective.
3. **Work within your Circle of Influence.**
4. **Make and keep commitments.** It's the clearest expression of proactivity and builds integrity.
5. **When you make a mistake:** acknowledge it, correct it, learn from it.

#### Discussion Questions: Habit 1

1. Frankl found freedom in the gap between stimulus and response. Describe a recent moment in your business where you **reacted instead of responded**. What would a two-second pause have changed about the outcome?
2. Pick one thing currently dominating your **Circle of Concern**, something you complain or worry about but don't control. What's one action in your **Circle of Influence** that touches the same issue?
3. Listen to your own language for a day. Write down three **reactive phrases** you caught yourself using ("I have to," "They make me," "If only…"). What's the proactive rewrite of each, and what would change if you actually spoke that way to your team?

### 7.5 Habit 2: Begin with the End in Mind

**Core framework.** **All things are created twice**: first mentally, then physically. Habit 2 is taking responsibility for the first creation instead of letting it happen by default through scripts inherited from family, culture, or circumstance.
- **Leadership** (doing the right things) comes before **management** (doing things right).
- Efficiency in climbing the ladder of success means nothing if the **ladder is leaning against the wrong wall**.

#### Signature Exercise: The Funeral Visualization

Imagine attending your own funeral three years from now. Four speakers give eulogies:
1. A family member
2. A close friend
3. A work colleague
4. Someone from your church or community organization

What would you want each to say about your character and contributions? The gap between that and your current behavior is the raw material for a personal mission statement.

#### Rescripting: Anwar Sadat

Sadat wrote about rescripting himself in a prison cell, shedding the script of hatred toward Israel his culture had handed him. His later peace initiative (which won the Nobel Peace Prize and ultimately cost him his life) is framed as the physical creation that followed a mental creation years earlier.

#### The Centers Analysis

Covey examines the possible centers people organize their lives around: **spouse, family, money, work, possessions, pleasure, friend, enemy, church, self**. Each produces instability in the four life-support factors:

| Factor | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Security** | Sense of worth, identity, emotional anchorage |
| **Guidance** | Source of direction |
| **Wisdom** | Perspective on life and balance |
| **Power** | Capacity to act |

Only a **principle-centered** life produces stability across all four, because principles don't shift with circumstances.

#### Mission Statements

- Covey shares examples of personal mission statements and extends the idea to **families and organizations**.
- **IBM** attributed part of its success to three articulated values (respect for the individual, excellence, service), and everyone in the company was trained in them.
- A mission statement only works if the people it governs **helped create it**.

#### Whole-Brain Approach

Covey draws on left-brain (logic, language, analysis) and right-brain (intuition, imagination, visualization) research to argue that **visualization and affirmation** are practical tools for writing mission statements and pre-living difficult moments.

#### Discussion Questions: Habit 2

1. Do the funeral exercise for real. Which of the four eulogies would be **easiest** to write about you as you are today, and which would be **hardest**? What does that gap tell you about where your time and attention actually go versus where you say they should?
2. Covey lists about ten **centers** people build their lives around. Be honest: what's actually at the center of yours right now? Use your **calendar and bank statement from the last 30 days** as evidence. What would shift if principles were at the center instead?
3. "All things are created twice." Pick a goal or project in your business right now. How clear is the **first (mental) creation**? Could you hand it to someone else and have them execute it? If not, what's missing?

### 7.6 Habit 3: Put First Things First

**Core framework.** Habit 3 is the physical creation: the second creation of Habit 2, and the moment-by-moment exercise of the independent will from Habit 1. It means organizing and executing around priorities set by principles and your mission, not by urgency or other people's agendas.

#### The Time Management Matrix

|  | **Urgent** | **Not Urgent** |
|--|-----------|----------------|
| **Important** | **Quadrant I: Necessity.** Crises, pressing problems, deadline-driven projects | **Quadrant II: Effectiveness.** Prevention, planning, relationship-building, recreation, recognizing new opportunities |
| **Not Important** | **Quadrant III: Deception.** Interruptions, some calls, mail, meetings, popular activities (urgency masquerading as importance) | **Quadrant IV: Waste.** Trivia, busywork, time wasters |

- Effective people spend most of their time in **Quadrant II**.
- Ineffective people bounce between Q1 (real crises) and Q3/Q4 (escape from crises).
- **Investing in Q2 shrinks Q1**, because prevention and planning head off crises.

#### The Executive Question

Covey asks groups of executives: what **one activity**, done consistently well, would have a significant positive impact on your personal or professional life? The answers are almost always Quadrant II. The follow-up: then why aren't you doing it?

#### Four Generations of Time Management

| Generation | Focus |
|------------|-------|
| 1st | Notes and checklists |
| 2nd | Calendars and appointment books |
| 3rd | Prioritization, goal-setting, daily planning |
| 4th (Covey's) | Managing **yourself**, not time, organized around **roles and principles** |

#### Weekly Organizing

Plan **weekly** rather than daily: a week is long enough to see all your roles and short enough to stay concrete.
1. Identify your roles (e.g., owner, manager, parent, spouse, community member).
2. Select two or three Quadrant II goals per role for the week.
3. Schedule those first.
4. Fit everything else around them.

The "big rocks" image (put the big rocks in the jar before the sand) is often used to teach this step. *(See verification note in Section 14.)*

#### Delegation: Gofer vs. Stewardship

- **Gofer delegation:** "Go for this, go for that," with step-by-step instructions; you supervise methods.
- **Stewardship delegation:** Focused on **results, not methods**. Requires mutual understanding of five elements:

| Element | Question It Answers |
|---------|---------------------|
| **Desired results** | What does done look like? |
| **Guidelines** | What are the boundaries and the no-go areas? |
| **Resources** | What help, money, and tools are available? |
| **Accountability** | How and when will results be evaluated? |
| **Consequences** | What happens (good and bad) as a result? |

**"Green and Clean":** Covey taught his seven-year-old son to care for the yard by defining the result ("green and clean"), the standards, and the accountability rather than dictating method.

#### Discussion Questions: Habit 3

1. Sketch the four-quadrant matrix and honestly estimate the percentage of your work hours **last week** in each quadrant. What's your **Q2 number**, and what would it take to double it over the next 90 days?
2. Covey's test: name one activity you know you should be doing consistently but aren't, that would have significant positive impact if you did. What's actually blocking it? (Hint: usually Q1 or Q3 crowding it out.)
3. Think of a task you delegated recently, or one you've refused to delegate. If you delegated it, was it **gofer-style or stewardship-style**? If you refused, which of the five stewardship elements (results, guidelines, resources, accountability, consequences) were you unwilling or unable to clarify, and what would it take to clarify them?

## 8. Interactive Class Tools

### 8.1 Circles of Control, Influence & Concern

| | |
|--|--|
| **File** | [`tools/circles-of-control/index.html`](tools/circles-of-control/index.html) (originally `circles_of_control.html`) |
| **Pairs with** | *7 Habits*, Habit 1 (Circle of Concern vs. Circle of Influence); Habit 1 Discussion Question 2 |
| **Origin** | Built from the blank "Circles of Control, Influence & Concern" template on the last page of a class PDF, converted into a fillable web page |
| **Tech** | Single standalone HTML file; vanilla JavaScript; CSS animations; Google Fonts (Caveat, DM Sans); no other dependencies |

**What it does:**
- Displays a **concentric-circles diagram** with three zones: **Control** (center), **Influence** (middle), **Concern** (outer).
- Each zone is **clickable** and has a matching **side panel**.
- Participants type items into an input field for each circle.
- Items appear as **animated note tags** both in the panel and as floating labels on the diagram.
- Individual items can be **deleted**.

**Suggested classroom use:**
1. Before class, have participants list everything currently weighing on them in their business.
2. In class, sort each item into Control, Influence, or Concern.
3. Discuss the three problem types (direct control → change habits; indirect control → change methods of influence; no control → change attitude).
4. Close with Habit 1 Discussion Question 2: one action in the Circle of Influence that touches an item in the Circle of Concern.
5. Optional follow-up: revisit the tool at the end of the 30-Day Proactivity Test to see whether the Circle of Influence grew.

The breakdowns in this repository were built from knowledge of the texts and supplemented with web research. They're strong teaching references, but specific names, numbers, and placements should be confirmed against the books before they appear on slides or handouts.

### General rule

Spot-check any **statistic, name, date, dollar figure, page number, or chapter placement** against the physical text before teaching it.

| Book | Item | Why It's Flagged |
|------|------|------------------|
